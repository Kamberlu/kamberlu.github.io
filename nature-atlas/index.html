NATURE ATLAS

What is happening to species, forests, wildlands, oceans and fresh water — what is driving it, and where protection and recovery stand. Every figure is the latest release of its source as of August 2026.

START HERE

What is this page? While the Carbon Atlas tracks what we put into the atmosphere, this page tracks what is happening to the living world — biodiversity, the variety and abundance of life on Earth. Wildlife populations, forests, wetlands, coral reefs and fish stocks are the planet's life-support infrastructure: they feed us, filter water, buffer storms, pollinate crops and store carbon.

One honest caveat before you read: unlike emissions, nature has no single unit of measurement. The numbers here come from different instruments measuring different things — how much average wildlife populations have shrunk, how many species are at risk of extinction, hectares of forest lost, the share of reefs heat-stressed. They are shown side by side, never added together. Each chart says exactly what its number does and does not mean.

How to use it: the page runs from bad news to good — species first, then forests and wildlands, then oceans and fresh water, then the ranked list of causes, and finally where protection genuinely works. Hover over any chart for details; a glossary near the bottom decodes the jargon, and every figure links to its public source.

Method & components
State scores stocks only, each 0–100 against the stated reference below, equal-weighted within each domain, then domains averaged — no defensible unequal weighting exists, so weights are transparent instead. Trend classifies each input's current direction on an ordinal scale (−2 rapidly worsening · −1 worsening · 0 flat · +1 stabilizing · +2 improving), which avoids unit-choice artifacts; flows (emission and loss rates) inform trend, not state. Acceleration is reported qualitatively by pressure type. The level is sensitive to reference choices (especially 450 ppm and the ~500 Gt budget); the trend column is robust to them. Vintages span 2018–2026. This is a summary lens over the sourced indicators of both atlases — not a measurement.

SPECIES DECLINE

How bad is it for wildlife? Two different lenses answer that, and they are often conflated. The Living Planet Index (left) tracks how much monitored animal populations have shrunk on average — a herd of 1,000 becoming 270 counts, even if the species survives. The IUCN Red List (right) counts how many entire species are threatened with extinction: currently 49,505 of 175,909 assessed — about 28%.

HOW TO READLeft chart: longer bars = steeper average population decline since 1970, split by region and by habitat type. Right chart: the share of each animal or plant group threatened with extinction — cycads (ancient palm-like plants) are the most endangered group on Earth. Neither number means "% of animals gone."

Living Planet Index, 1970–2020 — average population change by region & system

WWF / ZSL Living Planet Report 2024 (34,836 populations, 5,495 vertebrate species). An average of population trends — not "% of animals gone", and not extinctions.

Share of species threatened with extinction, by group

IUCN Red List v2026-1 (July 2026), best estimates. Insects have no comprehensive assessment — dragonflies & damselflies stand in. ~935 species recorded Extinct since 1500 (+81 extinct in the wild) — regarded as a severe undercount; 15 more were declared extinct in July 2026.

FORESTS & WILDLANDS

Two forest measures that shouldn't be mixed. Deforestation (left) means land permanently converted away from forest — usually to farms — as tracked by the UN's FAO: it is slowing, but still vast. Tree cover loss (right) is what satellites see disappear each year for any reason, including fire: 25.5 Mha globally in 2025, 42% of it fire. Beyond the forests: 75% of all land is significantly altered by humans, only ~23% remains as true wilderness, wetlands are down 22% since 1970 — and grasslands & wetlands are now being converted at four times the forest rate.

HOW TO READ"Mha" = million hectares; 1 Mha ≈ the area of Yellowstone plus Grand Teton. In the left chart, orange bars are forest destroyed per year and green bars are new forest growing back — the gap between them is the net loss. In the right chart, taller bars = worse years for untouched tropical rainforest ("primary" forest — the irreplaceable kind).

Deforestation is slowing — but far from stopped (Mha per year)

FAO Forest Resources Assessment 2025. Net change (after regrowth): −10.7 Mha/yr in the 1990s → −4.12 Mha/yr in 2015–2025. Forests still cover 4.14 bn ha — 32% of land — but 489 Mha net have been lost since 1990.

Tropical primary forest loss — landmark years

Global Forest Watch / Univ. of Maryland (published values; not a complete annual series). 2024's record was driven by fire; 2025 fell 36% yet remains 46% above 2015 and ~70% above the pace needed for zero deforestation by 2030.

What tropical deforestation is for

Attribution of tropical deforestation to commodities (Pendrill et al.-lineage attribution; shares of attributed loss, ~2001–2015 vintage — the standing decomposition). Of all permanent tree-cover loss 2001–2025, ~94% is agriculture (WRI/Google drivers dataset). Beef alone clears more tropical forest than palm oil, soy, wood, cocoa, coffee and rubber combined.

OCEANS & FRESH WATER

Fresh water is the hardest-hit realm in every dataset — rivers and lakes hold a tiny fraction of the world's water but a huge share of its endangered life. In the ocean, heat now rivals fishing as the top threat: the 2023–25 coral bleaching event (corals expelling the algae that feed them when water gets too warm) touched 84% of the world's reef area. The previous record event touched 68%.

HOW TO READLeft: the share of assessed fish stocks being caught faster than they can reproduce ("overfished") — nearly 4× worse than 1974. By volume the picture is less dire because most of what we actually land comes from well-managed stocks. Right: a scorecard of losses across ocean and freshwater ecosystems — longer bars are worse.

Marine stocks fished beyond sustainable levels

FAO SOFIA series (latest: SOFIA 2026, 2023 data). By volume the picture is better: 72.6% of landings come from sustainable stocks. Extremes: tuna ~99% of landings sustainable; Mediterranean & Black Sea majority overfished.

Ecosystem scorecards

Coral: NOAA CRW (Jun 2026), GCRMN 2020 (next global report due 2026), Eddy 2021, IPCC SR1.5. Mangroves: Global Mangrove Watch / State of the World's Mangroves 2024, IUCN 2024. Freshwater: LPR 2024; He et al. 2019; LPI migratory fishes 2024.

WHAT IS DRIVING THE LOSS

The UN's biodiversity science panel (IPBES) ranked the direct causes of nature's decline in its 2019 Global Assessment — still the standing ranking. On land the top driver is converting habitat to farmland; in the ocean, it's taking too much out. Climate change is third — but it is the fastest-rising, and unlike the others it cannot be fenced off by a protected area.

PROTECTION & RECOVERY

In 2022, nearly every country signed the Kunming–Montreal agreement, whose headline promise is 30×30: protect 30% of the world's land and 30% of its ocean by 2030. The bars show how far there is to go — coverage must roughly double on land and triple at sea in the four years remaining. The cards below are the counterweight to everything above: where conservation is funded and enforced, it measurably works.

Land & inland waters protected17.4% of 30%
Ocean protected10.0% of 30%
Bars show progress toward the 30×30 target (right edge = 30%). Protected Planet live statistics, August 2026 (ocean up from 8.4% in late 2024, helped by new large marine protected areas). Also under protection: ~20% of forests, >42% of remaining mangroves.
Read the wins honestly: they are proof that targeted conservation works, not that the trend has turned. Conservation since 1993 prevented an estimated 21–32 bird and 7–16 mammal extinctions — while the drivers above kept intensifying.

GLOSSARY

The jargon on this page, in plain words.

Biodiversity
The variety and abundance of life — from genes to species to whole ecosystems. More variety = more resilience.
Living Planet Index (LPI)
An average of trends across ~35,000 monitored vertebrate populations since 1970. "−73%" means the average population shrank by 73% — not that 73% of animals or species are gone.
IUCN Red List
The global inventory of extinction risk. Species are assessed into categories; "threatened" bundles the three worst: Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered.
Mha (million hectares)
The unit of land loss. 1 Mha = 10,000 km² ≈ the area of Yellowstone plus Grand Teton; 4.3 Mha ≈ Denmark.
Primary forest
Old, undisturbed native forest — the richest carbon and wildlife storehouse. Once cleared, it cannot be replanted back into existence on any human timescale.
Deforestation vs tree cover loss
Deforestation = land permanently switched away from forest (FAO). Tree cover loss = any tree canopy that disappears in a year, including fire and logging that may regrow (satellites).
Coral bleaching
When water stays too warm, corals expel the algae that feed and colour them, turning white. Bleached corals can recover if heat subsides quickly — or die if it doesn't.
Overfished stock
A fish population being caught faster than it can reproduce. "Sustainable" stocks can keep yielding indefinitely at current fishing pressure.
Mangrove
Coastal forests that grow in salt water — nurseries for fish, storm barriers for coasts, and carbon vaults several times denser than rainforest.
Wetland
Marshes, swamps, peatlands, floodplains. They cover ~6% of land but support ~40% of all species and store vast carbon.
30×30
The global target agreed in 2022: protect 30% of land and 30% of ocean by 2030.
IPBES
The UN's science panel for biodiversity — nature's equivalent of the IPCC climate panel.
